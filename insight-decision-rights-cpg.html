Decision Intelligence · Client Engagement

Perfectly designed to get the results it was getting

There's an old systems-thinking line: every system is perfectly designed to get the results it's getting. A multi-site beverage manufacturer's leadership team was well-intentioned and hardworking — and getting exactly the results their decision-making system was built to produce. Just not the results they wanted.

The assumption

Facing underperformance across sites, leadership's instinct was the one most leadership teams reach for first: the fix was more effort. Tighter execution. More accountability conversations. Sharper KPIs, posted more visibly, reviewed more often.

What was actually happening

Effort wasn't the constraint. Decisions across sites were running on local opinion and judgment — not because people lacked discipline, but because the information a decision-maker needed simply wasn't reaching them in a form or a timeframe that mattered for the decision in front of them. By the time the right data arrived, the decision had already been made on instinct.

Effort applied to the wrong structure just produces a more exhausted version of the same result.

Incentives, meanwhile, were quietly working against the outcome leadership actually wanted — rewarding what got measured at each site, not what genuinely drove performance across the network. Nobody had designed it to work that way. It had simply accumulated that way, one well-intentioned local decision at a time.

What we did

Rather than push harder against a structure working against itself, the engagement redesigned three things together, deliberately: decision rights — who actually has the authority to decide what, and at which level; information flow — getting the right data to the right decision-maker before the decision, not as a report afterward; and incentives — aligning what got rewarded with what actually mattered to the business, not just what was easiest to measure locally.

What changed

The result was $7M+ in tracked, validated savings across two years — delivered not by asking people to work harder inside the existing structure, but by changing the structure itself.

Most performance problems that present as an execution problem are actually a decision-architecture problem wearing an execution problem's clothes. Fixing the visible symptom without touching the structure underneath just resets the clock until the same result reappears.
